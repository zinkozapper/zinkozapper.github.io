## Overview

I've recently gotten some questions about my keyboard hence why this post. I've been using the [ZSA Moonlander](https://www.zsa.io/moonlander) as my primary work keyboard for a combined time of ~20 months (with a two year break during use). I'm going to cover my setup, how it's been for typing, and some repairs that I've been done.

So why did I choose the Moonlander?

### Backstory

About 4 years ago, I had a lot of pain in my wrists. I was probably typing on my computer for ~3-4 hours a day. After doing some research, I decided to use a different keyboard that was more ergonomic.

I settled on the Moonlander for a varity of reasons. At the time I still did a lot of gaming and so I wanted to have a high number of keys. I also wanted a wrist rest.

#### Split

Having a split keyboard allows you to position your arms a variable distance apart. This is very nice as it allows you to type in a more comfortable position.

One downside with this setup though is that the halves can move independantly. This can be quite annoying at times. At the moment I have a [3d printed fixed bracket](https://www.printables.com/model/153047-zsa-moonlander-fixed-bracket) to help accomdate this. You can easily adjust the size it is when printing to work specifically to your needs. I originally found the design on [ZSA's printables website](https://www.zsa.io/moonlander/printables). It has some pretty cool things.

#### Ortholinear

Look at your keyboard right now. Notice how the keys aren't in straight lines. Now look at your fingers. Does the shape of your fingers match what the keyboard looks like? Probably not. Ortholinear fixes this. All the keys are straight, just like your fingers! Crazy how that works.

## Typing

### The Workman Layout

Since I was already getting a new keyboard and would have to relearn how to type on it I decided to go all in and switch my keyboard layout. Ideally this would increase my speed and help again with ergonomics.

`Hey gamers, welcome back to my rant about why qwerty bad. So essentially qwerty go slow because it was designed to be slow for typewriters so that people didn't type too fast and jam typewriters. Very inefficient.`

I am using a custom modified version of the [workman layout](https://workmanlayout.org/). 

![Workman Layout](workmanLayout.png)

Brief history of keyboard layouts:
```
Qwerty (1870s) -> Dvorak (1930s) -> Colemak (pretty cool, 2006) -> Workman (slightly modified workman, 2010) -> Moonlander Workman (that I made, 2022).
```

### Learning a new layout

My biggest piece of advice for learning a new layout is that you need to be committed.

I personally don't think that everyone should go out switching and learning new layouts. It's too niche for the general populas.

It's very difficult and it can be infuriating at times. The first few weeks are the hardest but if you know what you're getting into, it's manageable.

For some reference on this timeline, my typing speed with a standard qwerty keyboard averaged ~90 wpm on a 15 second [Monkeytype test](https://monkeytype.com/).

To practice, I would often do about 15 minutes of monkeytype a day in addition to my standard work. I've since learned about a website called [keybr](https://www.keybr.com/) that I would use if I had to do it over again. I think it would have increased the improvement speed dramatically

``` Typing Speed Timeline
1 week (~10 wpm, 40% acc) -> 2 weeks (~15 wpm, 70% acc) -> 1 month (~ 50 wpm, 85% acc) -> 4 months (~70 wpm, 90% acc) -> 8 months (~80 wpm, 99% Acc) -> 10 months (~90 wpm, 99% Acc) -> 20 months (110 wpm, 99% Acc)
```

While this timeline is encouraging, it's still very hard. The biggest hump is getting past the first month as before that it's very difficult to do anything productive. Afterwards however, you're fast enough to be productive!

![Looking to install the Arch BTW OS](archbtw.jpg)

Another note, I don't think that anything 10 months and beyond was specific to the MWorkman layout. Rather, I think it was just my general typing speed increasing naturally as I spent more time practicing.

### Can you still use qwerty?

As I was researching keyboards, this was one thing that I was quite worried about. I had heard tales of people switching layouts and then no longer being able to type on qwerty.

The answer is yes! I've had no issues with this whatsoever. 

My theory is that since the Moonlander is so different then any standard qwerty keyboard, my muscle memory has tied itself to the specific keyboard and naturally defaults to a different layout for the different physical keyboards.

As a second witness for this, I've tried using standard workman on a normal keyboard and it doesn't work very well. I've also tried using qwerty on my workman keyboard and am not able to do it.

### Modified Layout

Here is a picture of what my MWorkman (Modified Workman) layout actually looks like. You'll notice I have some things changed specifically for me.

![MWorkman keyboard layout](keyboardLayer.png)

With all ZSA keyboards, you can create your own layout and then flash the keyboard firmware using their software called [Oryx](https://configure.zsa.io/)

In the standard workman layout, the `f` key is usually pressed by the right hand. For me, it was really hard to hit it and it caused a lot of strain. This made me move it to my left hand and it works much better.

`Meta + L` just locks my machine. It's the same as `Windows + L`.

The Tux icon to the left of that is my primary meta key.

Above my tab key it shows `Esc` and `Left Alt`. Why is this?

Well, you can make the keys do different things on a tap versus a press (and a few other things)!

This allows me to do some nifty things such as having tapping the key work escape but I can also use it as a keystroke combo to alt tab. Pretty cool.

### What about gaming?

![Gaming chair is the best workout machine](workout.png)

This was another one of my concerns. Will this keyboard work well with gaming?

The answer is kinda.

So, initially I was optimistic about it working well. I then remembered that I was no longer using qwerty.

This causes a big pain. Would I have to rebind every single game's qwerty bindings to something else? 

Luckily, no.

The keyboard has this exciting feature called virtual layers!

![Qwerty gaming layer in ZSA](gamingLayer.png)

I can make it so that I just click a button and it switches back to qwerty.

This works for pretty much every game. My muscle memory is also tied specifically to qwerty for gaming so I can't actually type normally with the qwerty mode.

It doesn't work too well for games in which you need both sides of the keyboard and the mouse (because it's split which increases left hand reach difference). My primary case for this is StarCraftII. 

## Repairs

About 3 years after buying the keyboard I started having issues with one of my TRRS ports (just the thing you plug your cable into). It would randomly just not connect and I put have to force it to stay at a plugged in at specifc angle or else one half of the keyboard would disconnect.

The official solution is to send your keyboard back since it has a 2 year warranty. Unfortunately, it had been a bit over 3 years by the time this happened.

Luckily, ZSA is a really cool company and strong proponent of right to repair!


![Right to Repair Meme](rightToRepair.jpg)

As it happens, ZSA happened to write a [blog post](https://blog.zsa.io/robins-repairs-trrs-port/) on how to DIY fix the problem I was having!

A few screws and a soldered connection later and we were tack in business. Took a total of ~20 minutes and I didn't have to ship my keyboard for warranty anywhere!
